# Express: Storyline

> Audience: Product team kickoff meeting
> Goal: Make the team *feel* the pain of onboarding so they prioritize fixing it

---

Marcus opens the email at 8:47am, still standing in the kitchen with his coffee getting cold. Three weeks into his new job as engineering lead, he finally got budget approval for the project management tool his team needs. The invite from his admin landed overnight.

He clicks the link on his phone, then stops himself — this deserves a proper screen. By the time he's at his desk, logged into his laptop, the email is buried under seventeen others. He scrolls. Scrolls again. Types "invite" into search. There it is.

The signup takes two minutes. Name, password, team name — "Platform Squad." He's in.

And then nothing.

An empty workspace stares back at him. The sidebar is dense — boards, timelines, documents, goals, sprints, automations, integrations, reports. Eight doors, no signs. Marcus has used project management tools before; he knows *conceptually* what a board is, what a sprint is. But he doesn't know what *this* tool's version of those things expects from him.

He clicks "Goals" because that feels strategic. He's an engineering lead; he thinks in goals. The page asks him to define an OKR structure, select a time horizon, connect to company objectives. He doesn't have company objectives set up. He doesn't even know if his VP uses this tool.

Back button.

He clicks "Sprints." It asks for iteration length, team velocity, a backlog. He has none of those things yet. He just wants to organize his team's work for *this week*.

Back button. The workspace is still empty. Forty seconds have passed[^1].

A small notification slides in from the bottom: "Tip: Start with a Board to organize your team's work." Marcus clicks it. Relief. A board template appears — To Do, In Progress, Done. He types "Fix auth timeout bug" into the first card. Then "Update API docs." Then "Interview candidate Thursday." 

Five minutes later he has twelve cards arranged across three columns. For the first time since opening the tool, he can see his week. He drags "Fix auth timeout bug" above "Update API docs." The tactile snap of drag-and-drop gives him a tiny hit of control.

But it's just him. Twelve cards on a board that only he can see.

He thinks about Wei, his senior engineer who always forgets verbal task assignments. About Priya, who asked last week if they could "just use a Trello or something." This board would actually help — if they were here.

He finds the invite flow. It asks him: Admin, Member, or Guest? For each person. What's the difference between Member and Guest? The tooltip says "Guests cannot create boards." But can they comment? Move cards? He doesn't know and the UI doesn't say.

He picks "Member" for everyone and pastes in five emails. A pause — the system validates seats. Send.

Then the waiting. He checks the board four times that afternoon. No new faces. He Slacks Priya: "hey did you get that invite?" She replies at 4pm: "oh yeah, I'll look at it tomorrow." 

Tomorrow comes. Wednesday. Thursday morning Wei posts a comment: "what do the labels mean?" Marcus smiles at his screen.

The tool is alive.

[^1]: Product analytics show 60% of new users take no action in the first 3 minutes after reaching the empty workspace (source: product-analytics-2026-Q1).
